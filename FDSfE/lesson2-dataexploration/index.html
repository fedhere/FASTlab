Lecture 2: Data Exploration¶

Introduction¶

In every facet of life, making effective decisions requires data. To understand and make use of data, you must thoroughly understand it.

One of the first stages of a data science project is Data Exploration. The basic way to explore a dataset is to look at the raw data in a table, but this approach --- while important --- is unlikely to reveal anything interesting. We must explore the data: understand how each of our variables may be related to one another. You can do this visually with a scatter plot, or you can calculate Pearson’s Correlation coefficient: a number that represents how related to variables are.

Python is the programming language of choice for data scientists, and as a result there already exists robust tools for data analysis. The first one we will look at is pandas.

Frequentist vs Bayesian interpretation of probability¶

  • Frequentist: “The fraction of times something happens is the probability of it happening.”

  • Bayesian: “I have a prior belief about whether the coin is fair (50/50 of getting heads/tail) or not.”

    • The Bayesian statistician could get 10 heads in a row, but if they have a prior belief that the coin is fair, then they will still expect to get 50% heads and 50% tails in the long run.

Probability Arithmetic¶

Rules¶

Probability is always between 0 and 1: 0<=P(A)<=1If Aˉ is the complement of A: P(A)+P(Aˉ)=1\begin{align*} \textrm{Probability is always between 0 and 1: }& 0 <= P(A) <= 1 \\ \textrm{If } \bar{A} \textrm{ is the complement of } A \textrm{: }& P(A) + P(\bar{A}) = 1 \\ \end{align*}Probability is always between 0 and 1: If Aˉ is the complement of A: ​0<=P(A)<=1P(A)+P(Aˉ)=1​
(3)

Disjoint events have independent probabilities.

Two disjoint events: the intersection (∩\cap∩) of probabilities of event A and B is 0.

If P(A)∩P(B)=0 then:P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)P(A∩B)=P(A)∗P(B)P(A∣B)=P(A)\begin{align*} \textrm{If }P(A) \cap P(B) = 0 \textrm{ then:}\\ P(A \cup B) &= P(A) + P(B) \tag{1}\\ P(A \cap B) &= P(A) * P(B) \tag{2}\\ P(A | B) &= P(A) \tag{3}\\ \end{align*}If P(A)∩P(B)=0 then:P(A∪B)P(A∩B)P(A∣B)​=P(A)+P(B)=P(A)∗P(B)=P(A)​(1)(2)(3)​
(4)

  1. The probability of A or B (A∪BA \cup BA∪B) happening is the sum of the probabilities of A and B.

  2. The probability of A and B (A∩BA \cap BA∩B) happening at once is the product of the probabilities of A and B.

  3. The probability of A happening, given that B has happened, is simply the probability of A happening because A and B are disjoint (independent).

Related events have dependent probabilities.

Two related events: the intersection (∩\cap∩) of probabilities of event A and B is greater than 0.

If P(A)∩P(B)>0 then:P(A∣B)=P(A∩B)P(B)P(A∣B)<P(A)P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B∣A)\begin{align*} \textrm{If }P(A) \cap P(B) > 0 \textrm{ then:}\\ P(A | B) &= \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \tag{4}\\ P(A | B) &< P(A) \tag{5}\\ P(A \cap B) &= P(A) P(B|A) \tag{6}\\ \end{align*}If P(A)∩P(B)>0 then:P(A∣B)P(A∣B)P(A∩B)​=P(B)P(A∩B)​<P(A)=P(A)P(B∣A)​(4)(5)(6)​
(5)

  1. The probability of A happening, given that B has happened, is the probability of A and B happening (A∩BA \cap BA∩B) divided by the probability of B happening by itself.

  2. The probability of A happening, given that B has happened, is always less than the probability of A happening by itself.

  3. The probability of A and B happening (A∩BA \cap BA∩B) is the probability of A happening by itself times the probability of B happening given A has happened.

Taxonomy¶

  • Distribution: A function that gives the probabilities of events ocurring.

  • Population: A set of similar objects or events that are of interest.

  • Sample: A finite subset of a population.

Pandas and the DataFrame¶

  • View a DataFrame with print() and __repr__ (a.k.a., what a DataFrame object returns)

  • df.shape: Knowing the shape of your data is of paramount importantance.

  • df.head() and df.tail(): Inspecting the first and last rows of your data can reveal something important for your analysis.

  • df.columns: This is the spine of your data. All analysis flows from knowing your columns (a.k.a, “features”).

  • df.info(): Another summary of your data.

  • df.describe(): A summary of your data.

  • df.corr()
